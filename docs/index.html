The question

What changes when characters interact?

A model can portray an individual fairly, yet distribute roles unequally when two people share a story.

Beyond isolated individuals

Most evaluations examine characters in isolation. GENRES places a male and a female character in the same social scenario, then asks a model to generate their profiles and a shared narrative.

Who takes action? Who is given higher status? Whose personality is developed? These relative portrayals reveal biases that individual descriptions can miss.

The preliminary single- versus dual-character comparison illustrates larger gaps in warmth-related descriptors and character depth in interpersonal settings.

The paper

Abstract

Ensuring the trustworthiness of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) requires rigorous evaluation of gender bias, particularly in socially sensitive domains. Existing benchmarks primarily assess bias in isolated contexts and are less suited to capturing biases that emerge through interpersonal interactions. We introduce GENRES, a benchmark for evaluating gender bias in MLLMs through the lens of social relationships in narrative generation. Grounded in Fiske's relational models theory, GENRES comprises 1,440 multimodal narrative elicitation pairs spanning four fundamental relationship types, diverse social scenarios, and three age groups. Its dual-character generation tasks elicit socially situated interpersonal narratives and enable fine-grained assessment across multiple dimensions of gender bias. Experiments on six recent MLLMs reveal persistent yet strongly relationship- and model-dependent gender disparities, with some patterns becoming more pronounced in interpersonal settings. We further evaluate two complementary generation-stage mitigation strategies. Our findings demonstrate the value of relationship-aware evaluation for uncovering subtle, interaction-driven gender bias in MLLMs.

Benchmark design

Social relationships as an evaluation lens

Grounded in Fiske’s relational models theory, GENRES pairs visual scenes with prompts for socially situated narrative generation.

01

Design the context

288 narrative-element entries span four relationships and three age groups, with conflict and cooperation scenarios.

02

Curate image–text pairs

GPT-4o prompts and SDXL images undergo CLIP screening and independent human verification. Five qualified images per entry form 1,440 NEPs.

03

Elicit and evaluate

Models generate two profiles and a shared story. Lexicon analysis, grammatical analysis and LLM judges measure relative gender portrayals.

Evaluation framework
Four dimensions. Eight complementary metrics.
Gender disparities appear in different forms. GENRES measures each dimension separately rather than combining them into a single fairness score.

 

 
01
Profile Assignment Bias
Which personality traits does the model give each character?
M1 Warmth Ratio
The proportion of warmth words among matched warmth and competence descriptors.
M2 Positive Score
The number of positive personality descriptors minus negative descriptors.

 
02
Agency and Role Bias
Who acts, and who occupies the higher-status role?
M3 Subject Ratio
Character-specific subject counts from subject–verb–object analysis.
M4 Status Allocation
Higher-status assignments, measured on unequal-status cases.

 
03
Emotional Expression Bias
How are emotion and positive sentiment distributed?
M5 Emotion Words
Emotion-related word counts from the NRC Emotion Lexicon.
M6 Positive Sentence
Positive-sentence ratios using a fine-tuned DistilBERT classifier.

 
04
Narrative Framing Bias
Who receives psychological depth and drives the narrative?
M7 Stereoscopicity
The presence of both positive and negative personality traits.
M8 Main Character
The character who addresses the conflict or leads cooperation.

 
Reading the scores. M1–M6 are male–female contrasts. M7 and M8 are Pearson correlations with gender (male = 1), and M8 includes only narratives with an identifiable protagonist. Positive values indicate male-favoring bias; negative values indicate female-favoring bias. Units differ across metrics.





 
Experimental findings
Bias depends on the model and the relationship
Six open- and closed-source MLLMs show persistent disparities. Greater model capability does not consistently imply smaller gender gaps.

Status allocation · M4
Models introduce hierarchies even in role-neutral contexts
Smaller models more often assign higher status to men, while GPT-4o and Gemini favor women. In Communal Sharing and Market Pricing, models introduce unequal status even though the relationships do not specify a hierarchy.
The figure retains samples with at least two-thirds agreement among evaluators. Gray marks equal-status assignments.

 
Narrative framing · M7 and M8
Being the protagonist does not guarantee a richer portrayal
Female characters more often drive the narrative across all six models. Meanwhile, Qwen-7B and GPT-4o more often give male characters both positive and negative traits, especially in Equality Matching and Market Pricing.
Mistral-Small-24B evaluator. Larger bubbles correspond to lower p-values; positive correlations indicate male-favoring bias.

 
Mitigation
Partial improvements, with room to go further
Fairness-Aware Prompting adds explicit generation-time fairness instructions. The Critique-and-Revision Framework uses iterative feedback to revise narratives. Gains depend on the model and metric; agency and emotional expression remain difficult to control through prompting alone.

Scope and limitations
GENRES uses paired male and female characters for controlled directional comparisons. It does not represent non-binary and gender-diverse identities. Names and scenarios primarily reflect Western/U.S. contexts. Results should be interpreted within this scope, and the benchmark should support broader analysis rather than serve as a standalone measure of fairness.
